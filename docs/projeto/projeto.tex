\begin{abstract}
This paper studies residential real estate price prediction in Japan using transaction-level data from the Ministry of
Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT). The problem is formulated as a supervised regression task in which
prices are estimated from spatial, temporal, accessibility, and physical attributes. The empirical workflow combines
chronological validation, leakage-aware preprocessing, model comparison, and interpretability analysis across the main
residential property types. The study compares tree-based ensembles, regularized linear regression, and support vector
regression. Gradient boosting outperforms the baselines. The predictions are strongest for pre-owned condominiums and
weakest for residential land, where location matters more than physical size.
\end{abstract}
     
\begin{resumo} 
Este trabalho investiga a predição de preços de imóveis residenciais no Japão a partir de dados transacionais do
Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT). O problema é formulado como uma tarefa de regressão
supervisionada, na qual os preços são estimados a partir de atributos espaciais, temporais, físicos e de acessibilidade.
O fluxo empírico combina validação cronológica, pré-processamento voltado à prevenção de vazamento de informação,
comparação de modelos e análise interpretável para os principais tipos de imóveis residenciais. O estudo compara
ensembles de árvores, regressão linear regularizada e regressão por vetores de suporte. O gradient boosting supera as
baselines. As previsões são mais precisas em condomínios usados e mais fracas em terrenos residenciais, nos quais a
localização pesa mais que o tamanho físico.
\end{resumo}


\section{Introduction}
The Japanese housing market presents an anomaly within global real estate economics. In most developed nations,
residential property functions as an appreciating asset and a means for generational wealth accumulation. In contrast,
housing in Japan behaves as a depreciating consumer durable. Within 20 to 30 years of construction, the structural value
of a typical Japanese home depreciates to virtually zero. This structural depreciation creates a stark institutional
decoupling between land value and building value \cite{yoshida2016structure}. While urban land, especially within the
Tokyo and Osaka metropolitan areas, retains substantial value and responds to conventional market forces, the structures
erected upon that land are treated as temporary assets. According to data from the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure,
Transport and Tourism (MLIT), the secondary housing market accounts for only approximately 15\% of total housing
transactions in Japan, compared to over 80\% to 90\% in the US and UK \cite{mlit2019revitalization}.

This environment traces its origins back to the post-World War II reconstruction era. Facing severe housing shortages
and rapid urbanization, the Japanese government prioritized speed and quantity over long-term structural durability
\cite{hirayama2006housing}. The resulting regulatory framework and market incentives codified a system of high-turnover
construction, which was later exacerbated by the asset price bubble of the late 1980s. Japan’s geographic vulnerability
to seismic activity also dictates its building philosophies. The Building Standard Act of Japan has undergone continuous
revisions, with the most critical shift occurring in 1981 with the introduction of the \textit{Shin-Taishin} New
Anti-Seismic Design Code). Homes constructed prior to 1981 are fundamentally viewed as structurally unsafe and
frequently fail to qualify for standard bank financing, as they're heavily penalized under modern insurance structures.
Subsequent amendments following the 1995 Hanshin-Awaji (Kobe) earthquake and the 2011 Tohoku earthquake have further
accelerated the technological obsolescence of older properties. Consequently, buyers often perceive purchasing a
pre-owned home not just as an economic compromise, but as a direct compromise on structural safety.

Complementing these rigid safety mandates is a pervasive cultural preference for new builds. A newly built home carries
a significant socio-cultural premium in Japan, symbolizing clean slates, modernization, and social status. This
preference is structurally reinforced by financial institutions, whereas Japanese banks routinely structure residential
mortgages around the statutory useful life of the building. For a standard timber-frame home, this period is legally
defined as 22 years. Once a structure surpasses this threshold, securing a mortgage for its resale becomes exceedingly
difficult, effectively freezing the secondary market.

It is also important to note that one of the most pressing modern challenge facing the Japanese housing market is the
nation's demographic trajectory. Japan is characterized by a rapid aging society combined with a beneath replacement
fertility rate, a critical example of most modern post-industrial societies. The population has been in a continuous
state of decline since its peak in the late 2000s. This population contraction has triggered the abandoned house
(\textit{akiya}) phenomenon. Millions of homes across rural, suburban, and even peripheral urban areas stand completely
vacant, given the tendency of inheriting families to frequently abandon the physical structures. This has created a
massive inventory of valueless, deteriorating properties that distort local real estate valuations and create severe
municipal management challenges.

The Japanese housing market presents an invaluable case study for urban economists and sociologists alike. It challenges
the universal assumption that residential property is an inherently safe, appreciating asset class. As other
post-industrial nations begin to face similar demographic plateaus and climate-induced regulatory pressures,
understanding the mechanisms of Japan’s ``scrap-and-build'' housing model becomes essential. 

\section{Research Problem and Objectives}
This work investigates the extent to which transaction-level information can explain and predict residential real estate
prices in Japan. Using data collected by the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) between 2005
and 2019 and made available through Kaggle \cite{nishiodens_japan_real_estate}, the study frames Japanese housing
valuation as a supervised regression problem. The central task is to estimate property transaction prices from
observable attributes such as location, land characteristics, building age, and temporal market conditions.

The problem is relevant because Japanese real estate prices are shaped by a combination of economic, demographic and
geographic factors that differ from those observed in many other developed housing markets. In this context, predictive
modeling is not only a technical exercise, but also a way to examine which variables carry the strongest explanatory
power in a market characterized by structural depreciation, regional imbalance, and changing demand. Accordingly, this
paper aims to develop, evaluate, and interpret Machine Learning regression models capable of predicting housing prices
while providing insight into the main determinants of valuation in the Japanese residential market.

This study followed a reproducible empirical workflow that combined data cleaning, feature preparation, model
comparison, and interpretability analysis. Several regression algorithms with distinct inductive biases, including
Gradient Boosting ElasticNet regression, and Support Vector Regression, were trained and evaluated under the same
temporal validation strategy. Rather than estimating a single pooled model for all observations, the empirical design
separated the task into independent studies for the main residential property types. The models were trained and
evaluated under the same temporal validation strategy within each study. Their predictive performance was compared using
standard regression metrics, while feature importance analysis was used to relate model behavior back to the economic
and spatial determinants of Japanese housing prices.

\section{Exploratory Data Analysis}
The exploratory analysis began with an inspection of the structure, completeness, and temporal distribution of the MLIT
transaction dataset. Each record describes a real estate transaction in Japan, with \textit{TradePrice} defined as the
target variable to be predicted. The explanatory variables combine geographic identifiers, such as prefecture,
municipality, district, and nearest station, with accessibility measures, including the minimum and maximum travel time
to the closest station.

The dataset also includes physical property attributes, such as area, land shape, frontage, floor plan, building year,
structure, total floor area, and renovation status, as well as urban-planning information, including zoning, coverage
ratio, floor-area ratio, road direction, and road classification. Temporal variables, represented by transaction year,
quarter, and period, were used to examine the distribution of observations over time and to support the chronological
modeling strategy adopted later in the pipeline. This initial analysis also guided the treatment of missing values,
redundant variables, and features that could leak information from the target.

The correlation analysis in Figure~\ref{fig:feature-correlation} highlights the strongest linear relationships between
\textit{TradePrice} and the available numerical attributes. Property size, represented by \textit{Area} and
\textit{TotalFloorArea}, has a clear positive association with transaction price, confirming that larger properties tend
to command higher market values. Road frontage and other access-related variables are also related to price, suggesting
that physical accessibility and road exposure contribute to property valuation. One less intuitive result is the weak
negative correlation between \textit{BuildingYear} and \textit{TradePrice}, since newer buildings are usually expected
to be more valuable. This pattern may reflect the fact that many older properties in the dataset are larger, renovated,
or located in more established and expensive urban areas. The following plots examine these hypotheses by looking at the

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Data Cleaning and Feature Preparation}
Data cleaning was performed only after the chronological train-validation-test split, so that all preprocessing
parameters were estimated from past observations and then applied to later periods. This design reduced the risk of
information leakage from future transactions into the training process. SQLite was used as a lightweight database layer
to organize the raw files and enable efficient access to the large dataset directly within the code. The first step
removed variables that were either not suitable for modeling or directly derived from the target. The textual
\textit{Remarks} field was discarded because of its unstructured nature and high missingness, same as for
\textit{Renovation}, while \textit{UnitPrice} and \textit{PricePerTsubo} were removed because they encode
transformations of \textit{TradePrice} and would therefore leak information about the response variable.

Missing values were then treated through a combination of domain rules and train-fitted statistical imputations. For
property types in which some attributes were conceptually absent, such as land-only, condominium, or rural transactions,
variables including floor plan, structure, road classification, city planning, frontage, breadth, and nearest station
were filled as not applicable or with structurally meaningful values. In particular, frontage and road breadth were set
to zero for condominiums, where these measurements do not translate to the category. The original station-distance field
was also reconstructed from the minimum and maximum time-to-station variables, producing a consistent numerical
accessibility measure. Remaining numerical gaps, including frontage, breadth, coverage ratio, and floor-area ratio, were
imputed with statistics learned exclusively from the training set, primarily district-level aggregates with global
medians as fallbacks. Remaining missing values in structure, road classification, and city planning were kept as an
explicit unknown category before categorical encoding. Because the MLIT transaction records do not include geographic
coordinates, latitude and longitude were obtained from an auxiliary source, the Geolonia Japanese address dataset. Each
transaction was matched to a coordinate pair by combining Prefecture, Municipality, and District, yielding
district-level \textit{Latitude} and \textit{Longitude} features that were merged into the modeling tables. Missing
coordinates after this merge were handled with municipality-level medians, again fitted only on the training partition
and then applied consistently to the validation and test sets.

Additional feature preparation was applied after the initial cleaning stage. The original station-distance
representation was converted into a numerical \textit{TimeToNearestStation} variable using the midpoint between the
minimum and maximum reported travel times, while the transaction quarter was encoded with sine and cosine
transformations to preserve its cyclical structure. The target variable was transformed with $\log(1+x)$ because housing
prices are strongly right-skewed, with a small number of very expensive transactions that can dominate loss functions
based on absolute or squared errors. Modeling the logarithm of price compresses these extreme values and makes the
learning problem closer to predicting relative price differences rather than only fitting the largest transactions. The
models were therefore trained and produced predictions on the transformed scale. Before reporting evaluation metrics,
both predictions and observed values were converted back to Yen using the inverse transformation. The final feature set
combined numerical attributes (\textit{TimeToNearestStation}, \textit{Area}, \textit{BuildingYear},
\textit{CoverageRatio}, \textit{FloorAreaRatio}, \textit{Year}, cyclical quarter encodings, \textit{Latitude},
\textit{Longitude}, \textit{Frontage}, and \textit{Breadth}) with one-hot encoded categorical attributes for
\textit{Structure}, road \textit{Classification}, and \textit{CityPlanning}. Finally, numerical features were normalized
with train-fitted min-max scalers, and feature names were standardized to ensure compatibility with the different
modeling libraries.

\subsection{Property-Type Study Design}
The final modeling pipeline was organized as a set of independent property-type studies. Three residential market
segments were considered: \textit{Residential Land (Land and Building)}, \textit{Residential Land (Land Only)}, and
\textit{Pre-owned Condominiums, etc.}. Agricultural and forest land observations were excluded from this stage because
their price formation mechanisms differ substantially from residential transactions and would introduce a distinct
economic problem into the same regression task.

For each study, the dataset was first filtered to the corresponding property type and then passed through the complete
workflow of chronological splitting, cleaning, feature engineering, scaling, hyperparameter optimization, training,
evaluation, and interpretability analysis. This avoids forcing structurally heterogeneous markets into a single
regression function. It also prevents the property-type indicator from acting as a coarse proxy for different valuation
regimes, as we've noted in the preliminary tests, where the \textit{Type} would act was one the of the main indicators
of value. The same experimental setup was applied to all studies, while parameter files, diagnostic plots, input
snapshots, and metric records were stored separately or tagged by study name to preserve traceability.

\subsection{Models and Evaluation}
The study compared four regression models with different assumptions and learning architectures. \textit{XGBoost}
\cite{chen_xgboost} was used as a gradient boosting model that builds weak decision trees sequentially, using previous
errors to construct a stronger ensemble, while \textit{LightGBM} \cite{lightgbm} provided a more computationally
efficient boosting approach based on leaf-wise tree growth. As a linear baseline, \textit{ElasticNet} \cite{Zou2005}
combined L1 and L2 regularization to balance feature selection and coefficient shrinkage. Finally, \textit{Support
Vector Regression} \cite{Smola2004} estimated a regression function within an error-tolerant margin, allowing nonlinear
patterns to be modeled through kernel transformations.

Model performance was evaluated using complementary regression metrics on both the transformed and original price
scales. In log space, Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and the coefficient of determination ($R^2$) were used to compare
models on the same scale used during training. After reversing the logarithmic target transformation, RMSE and Mean
Absolute Error (MAE) measured absolute deviations in Yen, while original-scale $R^2$ summarized the proportion of price
variance explained by each model.

Reproducibility was addressed by centralizing the experimental configuration, fixing random seeds for Python and NumPy,
and applying the same chronological split, preprocessing rules, scaling, and model evaluation procedure across all
algorithms and property-type studies. The pipeline also generated a run identifier and stored metadata, model input
splits, and training, validation, and test metrics, allowing each experiment to be traced back to the study, data
partitions, and feature set used during training.

Interpretability was incorporated after model selection (\textit{post hoc}) in order to connect predictive performance
with substantive housing-market explanations. For the best-performing model, global feature importance was used to
identify the variables that contributed most strongly to prediction across the test set.

\section{Results}
Across all three markets, the gradient boosting models (GBMs) consistently outperformed the linear and kernel baselines.
\textit{ElasticNet} and \textit{Support Vector Regression} achieved substantially higher error on both the
log-transformed and original Yen scales and struggled to reproduce the wide, right-skewed distribution of transaction
prices. On the test set, their predictions failed to track the observed price range, either compressing values near the
lower end of the distribution or producing unstable extrapolations for higher-priced transactions.

plot for the baseline models on the held-out test period in the \textit{Residential Land (Land and Building)} study, a

Comparing the feature importance profiles between these distinct market segments highlights why the condominium models
achieve superior predictive power. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:feature-importance}, valuation dynamics differ
fundamentally between property types. For \textit{Pre-owned Condominiums} (typically Japanese apartments), a single
structural attribute, \textit{Area}, dominates the model's decision-making process, capturing the core variance of the
problem and enabling clean generalization. That is, the asset class has a more predictable, standardized pricing
mechanism.

In contrast, for \textit{R. Land (Land and Building)}, physical dimensions alone are insufficient. Instead, spatial
coordinates (\textit{Latitude} and \textit{Longitude}) emerge as primary drivers, confirming that real estate valuation
in this segment is very sensitive to location. Since modeling localized geographical premiums and complex neighborhood
dynamics are inherently more difficult than scaling prices by floor area, the models struggle to find an optimal global
solution, resulting in the higher error metrics observed in Table~\ref{tab:test-metrics}.

\section{Conclusion}
This study has investigated the task of predicting residential real estate transaction prices in Japan, a market
characterized by a unique decoupling of structural and land values driven by strict anti-seismic mandates, cultural
fresh-build preferences, and stark demographic contractions. Our empirical findings demonstrate that real estate
valuation in this environment is an inherently complex problem. The predictive difficulty is heavily
stratified across property types. That is, while condominium valuations scale predictably with physical size, standalone
residential properties depend on localized geographical premiums that are notoriously difficult to capture using global
estimators. 

The primary insight of this work is that confronting this inherent difficulty requires a strategic decomposition of the
market. By segmenting the macro-level dataset into isolated, homogeneous property-type studies, we prevented structural
differences from muddying the models' inductive biases. This design paved a clear path toward localized optimization,
yielding highly viable predictions in the condominium sector and establishing baselines for land transactions. 

To bridge the remaining performance gaps in the more volatile residential land segments, future research should
transition from global spatial coordinates to richer localized indicators. Integrating localized features, such as
moving historical neighborhood averages, district-level socioeconomic indices, and granular accessibility metrics, will
allow the models to better map complex spatial dynamics. Ultimately, this combination of targeted data segmentation and
localized feature engineering offers a reproducible framework for executing predictive modeling inside highly
non-traditional asset markets.
